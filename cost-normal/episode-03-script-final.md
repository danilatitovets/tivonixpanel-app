# Cost of Normal — Episode 03

## Canonical title candidate
You Didn’t Crash. So Why Did Your Car Insurance Go Up?

## Final fact-checked narration

You open your car-insurance renewal and the number is higher again.

But you didn’t crash. You didn’t get a speeding ticket. You didn’t add a teenage driver. Maybe nothing about the way you drive changed at all.

So why did the price change?

Because your insurance premium is not just a score for your personal driving history. It is a price for future risk — and some of that risk has almost nothing to do with what happened to you last year.

Start with the car itself.

Modern vehicles can be safer and more capable, but they can also be more complicated to repair. A minor collision that once meant replacing a bumper can now involve cameras, radar, parking sensors, wiring and calibration. That does not mean every modern car is expensive to fix, or that every sensor turns a small accident into a huge bill. It means the cost of returning some vehicles to pre-crash condition has more components than it used to.

AAA documented this in a 2023 study of three model-year-2023 vehicles. In the specific crash scenarios it tested, damaged advanced driver-assistance components and their calibration could account for as much as 37.6 percent of the total repair cost. That is not a universal 2026 price quote. It is evidence of how sensing and calibration can add another layer to modern collision repair.

And the broader repair bill is still moving. In August 2026, the U.S. Consumer Price Index for motor-vehicle maintenance and repair was 5.2 percent higher than a year earlier. Maintenance and servicing was up 7.9 percent, while the narrower motor-vehicle repair category was up 2.6 percent.

That matters because insurance ultimately pays claims in dollars. If covered repairs become more expensive, the expected cost of insuring a pool of vehicles can rise even if accident frequency does not rise by the same amount.

Then there is the part of an auto claim that has nothing to do with sheet metal.

Liability coverage can pay for injuries. Medical treatment, lost income, legal expenses and settlements can make a serious crash far more expensive than the physical damage to the cars involved. An Insurance Research Council study released in 2026, using claim data from 2017 through 2022, found average bodily-injury claim payments rising from about fourteen thousand dollars to more than twenty thousand dollars. Attorney involvement also increased. Those are historical observations, not a claim about the cost of every accident today. But they show why insurers care about claim severity, not simply accident counts.

Now zoom out from your car to your ZIP code.

Insurance is built around risk pooling. Your driving record matters, but so can where the vehicle is garaged, what model it is, local crash experience, theft exposure, weather losses and the insurer’s experience with similar policies.

That is why two people who both say, “I haven’t had an accident,” can still receive very different prices.

Take theft. Vehicle theft surged earlier in the decade, but this is exactly where the current data requires some nuance. The National Insurance Crime Bureau reported 659,880 vehicle thefts in 2025 — down 23.2 percent from 2024 and the lowest level in several decades.

So it would be wrong to say your premium is rising because theft is still exploding everywhere.

But national averages are not local risk. Theft exposure remains different across places and vehicle models. An insurer pricing a specific car in a specific location is not underwriting the national average. It is estimating the expected losses attached to that policy and to a larger book of similar risks.

Weather works in a similar way.

A driver can go years without causing a collision and still live in an area exposed to hail, flooding, wildfire, severe storms or other events that damage many vehicles at once. Comprehensive claims do not require you to have made a driving mistake. When loss experience changes across a region, that can become part of the pricing pressure insurers try to reflect in rates.

But insurers cannot necessarily change every price instantly or arbitrarily.

Auto insurance is regulated at the state level. Rules differ by jurisdiction, but insurers may have to file proposed rate changes and supporting actuarial information with regulators. Public systems such as SERFF and state insurance-department databases can make parts of that process visible.

This creates an important timing problem.

Claims can become more expensive first. Insurer losses deteriorate. Carriers then seek or implement higher rates under the rules that apply in each state. Those higher premiums can remain visible even after some of the original pressure begins to cool.

The industry data shows that cycle.

In the first half of 2025, the National Association of Insurance Commissioners reported private-passenger auto liability direct written premiums 6.1 percent above a year earlier, while auto physical-damage premiums were up 3.6 percent. Direct written premium growth is not the same thing as the rate increase on an average individual policy. But NAIC also reported improved underwriting results and greater capacity and competition, conditions it said likely caused insurers to pull back from the scale of the large rate increases seen earlier.

And by 2026, something even more interesting happened.

The national insurance number started going down.

In August 2026, the Consumer Price Index for motor-vehicle insurance was 5.1 percent lower than a year earlier. It fell 0.8 percent in August alone on a seasonally adjusted basis.

At the exact same time, motor-vehicle maintenance and repair was still 5.2 percent more expensive than a year earlier.

That is the part of this story that is easy to miss.

The forces underneath an insurance premium do not all move together, and they do not all reach your renewal at the same moment.

Repair costs can be rising while the national insurance CPI is falling. Theft can decline nationally while one city or one model remains unusually risky. An insurer can improve its overall profitability while still changing rates for a particular territory or group of vehicles.

And your own clean record can remain completely true through all of it.

There is one more distinction that matters: the national CPI is an average measure of consumer prices. It is not a promise about what happens to your next renewal. Your carrier, state, vehicle, coverage and rating factors can move differently from the national index.

None of this means every premium increase is automatically justified. Pricing models differ. Insurers compete differently. State rules differ. Your coverage, deductibles, discounts, credit-related insurance variables where legally permitted, annual mileage and vehicle can all change the result. Shopping the same coverage across carriers can reveal very different prices for the same household.

It also does not mean insurance companies are simply victims of costs they cannot control. They choose underwriting strategies, expense structures and pricing decisions, and regulators sometimes challenge proposed increases.

The point is narrower.

When your renewal rises and you say, “But I didn’t crash,” you are answering only one question the insurance price is trying to estimate.

Your history is part of the equation.

The car is part of it.

The cost of fixing it is part of it.

The injuries a future crash might cause are part of it.

Your location is part of it.

And the loss experience of thousands of other drivers can be part of it too.

That is what insurance does: it turns a pool of uncertain future losses into a price today.

Sometimes that price goes up even when you did nothing wrong.

And, as the 2026 data now shows, sometimes the national price can start coming back down even while some of the costs underneath it are still rising.

The renewal notice only shows you the number.

The real story is everything that went into it.
